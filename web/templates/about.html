Why it exists

Claims like "banned in Europe but allowed in America" spread fast, and they are hard to check: every jurisdiction publishes its rules in a different format, with different names and numbers for the same substance. The answers exist, but they are scattered across legal texts, registers, spreadsheets and PDFs. This site gathers them, matches them up, and shows the result in plain language — with a link to the official text behind every status.

Principles

  • Official sources only. Every status comes from a government or intergovernmental publication, downloaded automatically. Nothing is copied from blogs, apps or campaign lists.
  • Show the source. Every status links to the text it comes from, and says how the match was made.
  • No guessing. If a substance cannot be matched to a source, the site says No data. Uncertain cases are labelled as uncertain, not rounded up or down.
  • Legal facts, not safety verdicts. A status says what a list allows. It does not say whether a substance is safe or harmful.
  • Independent and free. No ads, no sponsors, no affiliate links, no paid tiers. Not affiliated with any government, agency or company.
  • Private. No cookies, no accounts, no tracking. Details on the legal page.

How it works

  1. ⬇️DownloadEvery week an automated job downloads each official source and keeps a dated, checksummed copy.
  2. 🔎ReadParsers read the legal texts, registers, spreadsheets and PDFs, and pull out each additive's entry.
  3. 🧩MatchEntries are joined across sources by E/INS number, Colour Index, CAS number and normalised names.
  4. ✅CheckCounts, required fields and known reference additives are verified. Anything suspicious stops the update.
  5. 🚀PublishThe site is rebuilt and changes are recorded. If a check fails, the last good data stays online.

How additives are matched across sources

Each source names additives differently. The EU and UK use E-numbers, Australia and New Zealand use INS numbers, the FDA uses chemical names, CAS and Colour Index numbers, and Health Canada uses names only. Records are joined in this order:

  1. E-number or INS number (EU list, GB register, Food Standards Code schedules).
  2. Colour Index number for colours (from the EU specifications, Regulation (EU) No 231/2012, and the FDA inventory's alternative names).
  3. CAS registry number, where the EU specifications give one.
  4. Name after normalisation (case, punctuation, plural forms, British and American spelling, "FD&C" spellings), using the official names and the synonyms in the EU specifications.
  5. A small reviewed match table for names that cannot be matched automatically, for example where the INS code in Australia/New Zealand groups several EU sub-numbers.

US revocations that are newer than FDA's inventory are taken from final orders in the Federal Register — for example, the revocation of FD&C Red No. 3 for food, effective 15 January 2027. The EU history is built by comparing every consolidated version of the EU list since June 2013.

If a record cannot be matched, the site shows No data instead of guessing. On each additive page, a note under each status says how the match was made.

How the data stays current

  • Every Monday an automated job downloads each source again and records its size, checksum and time. A source counts as changed only when its content changes, not when the page around it does.
  • The new data is parsed and checked: expected fields must be present, counts must not drop sharply, and reference additives (such as E 129 and E 171) must still come out right.
  • If a check fails, the site keeps showing the last data that passed, and the problem is reported automatically. Broken data is not published.
  • Status differences between the new and the previous version are listed on the changes page, with the date they were detected.

What this site does not cover

  • Not legal advice. Statuses are simplified summaries. Conditions of use (which foods, what maximum levels, purity criteria, labelling duties) are shown only in part. Always check the official source.
  • US state laws (for example state laws on food dyes) are not included: there is no structured official source for them. The US column shows federal status only.
  • GRAS substances used in the US without notification to the FDA are not in the FDA inventory and cannot be shown.
  • Safety values such as the acceptable daily intake (ADI) are not shown.
  • Flavourings, enzymes, processing aids and nutrients are covered only where they appear in the additive lists used here.
  • Other countries. Japan, China and India publish additive lists too, but not under terms that clearly allow this site to reuse and republish them automatically. They are left out rather than covered badly.
  • Northern Ireland follows EU food additive rules; see the EU column. New Zealand's food law adopts the joint Food Standards Code, so Australia and New Zealand share a column.
